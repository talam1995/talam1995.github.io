I am a PhD student in Computer Science at New Mexico State University and a Graduate Research Assistant in the NMSU Research Cores Program. My work sits at the intersection of statistics and computing, with a focus on population-level human digital twins.

## Research

My dissertation research explores how real-world survey data can be used to build reliable, population-level representations of people for studying policy questions. Broadly, the work involves:

- **Survey calibration:** adjusting survey samples so they better reflect the population they represent.
- **Synthetic populations:** generating privacy-preserving synthetic data that retains key statistical relationships.
- **Causal modeling:** using structural causal models to explore counterfactual "what-if" scenarios.
- **Validation and uncertainty:** assessing how errors at each stage affect the reliability of the results.

## Background

My training is in statistics. At the University of Dhaka, where I earned a B.S. in Statistics, my undergraduate project on divorce in Bangladesh gave me my first hands-on experience with the full cycle of survey research: designing a questionnaire, collecting primary data, and modeling the results.

I then spent three and a half years as an Actuarial Associate at the Syngenta Foundation for Sustainable Agriculture in Bangladesh. There I led the development of weather index insurance products for farmers, turning satellite and weather-station data into premiums and payouts, and working with international consultants and local stakeholders to get products into the field.

I returned to academia for an M.S. in Statistics and Data Science at The University of Texas at El Paso. My [thesis](https://scholarworks.utep.edu/open_etd/4219/), supported by the NSF LEAPS-MPS project, studied optimal prediction of future failure times from censored life-testing data and was nominated for the College of Science best thesis award. I also taught as a graduate teaching assistant in the Department of Mathematical Sciences. As a research associate at UTEP afterward, I applied statistics to public health informatics training, groundwater risk assessment with El Paso Water, and mobility research in stroke and aging populations.

At NMSU, alongside my dissertation research, I write training guides and documentation that help researchers across the university use the Discovery high-performance computing cluster.

The common thread across this work is imperfect real-world data (surveys, sensors, weather records) used to support real decisions. That is what draws me to my current research.
